B000: MPSMA-DEIM with a Multi-branch Progressive Stem and Multi-scale Context-aware Attention for UAV Small Object Detection
B001: Peng Sheng*, Jiwei Fang, Wenhao Jiang, Jun Hu, Zixing Wang
B002: School of Artificial Intelligence and Big Data, Hefei University, Hefei 230601, Anhui, China
B003: *Corresponding author: CppJava@163.com
B004: Abstract
B005: Small objects in unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) images provide limited visual evidence and occur amid clutter and scale variation. We present MPSMA-DEIM, an adaptation of DEIM-D-FINE that combines a Multi-branch Progressive Stem (MPS) with Multi-scale Context-aware Attention (MSCA). The stem merges detail, downsampling and high-frequency branches before producing stride-four features. The attention design uses pooling-based context at an intermediate stage and dilated context at the deepest stage, with energy-based gates derived from SimAM. In the recorded local COCO-style evaluations of the VisDrone2019 validation set, the complete n configuration achieves average precision (AP) of 0.2270 and small-object AP of 0.1518; the s configuration achieves AP of 0.2876. These results describe individual configurations rather than isolated component effects: the historical baseline uses a different batch size, and some variants also change the aggregation structure. A matched baseline comparison is needed to determine whether this combination improves detection under the same training and evaluation conditions.
B006: Keywords: UAV imagery; small object detection; multi-branch progressive stem; multi-scale context-aware attention; DEIM-D-FINE
B007: 1. Introduction
B008: Unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) imagery poses a challenging object-detection setting because many objects occupy few pixels, overlap with neighbouring objects, and appear against complex backgrounds [1], [2]. Small-object performance depends on both the features retained during early downsampling and the context available for classification and localization. These considerations motivate changes to feature extraction, but an architectural rationale alone does not establish that a particular component improves detection.
B009: Existing approaches combine local and contextual information in different ways. Liang et al. integrate convolutional local-context enhancement with a Swin Transformer for UAV imagery [3]. For detection transformers, DEIM improves training through dense matching and a matchability-aware loss [4], while D-FINE refines bounding-box distributions across decoder layers [5]. These methods provide the host architecture and training framework for this work. We investigate a narrower design question: how to combine a shallow branch for fine detail with context-dependent feature weighting inside this backbone.
B010: The architectural contribution of MPSMA-DEIM is the combination of two concrete modifications. The Multi-branch Progressive Stem (MPS) aligns three shallow feature paths, applies residual spatial weighting, and fuses them before the final stem downsampling. Multi-scale Context-aware Attention (MSCA) uses different gates across backbone stages, including a pooled-context gate and a dilated-context gate. The constituent operations and SimAM-style weighting are inherited techniques. We also use the sigmoid-weighted linear unit (SiLU), without claiming a new activation mechanism. We report traceable configuration-level results, distinguish the n and s protocols, and identify the comparisons that require matched controls before a causal performance claim can be made.
B011: 2. Related Work
B012: Real-time detectors seek a useful balance between detection accuracy and computation. YOLOv10 uses consistent dual assignments to support inference without non-maximum suppression (NMS) [6]. DEIM [4] and D-FINE [5] instead develop the training and localization of detection transformers. MPSMA-DEIM retains the encoder and decoder design of its corresponding DEIM-D-FINE scale and modifies the backbone. Results obtained with different data splits, initializations or inference implementations cannot by themselves establish a better accuracy-speed trade-off.
B013: Feature recalibration and multi-scale aggregation are established design tools. Effective squeeze-excitation (ESE), introduced with VoVNetV2 in CenterMask [7], uses channel-wise recalibration. SimAM [8] derives neuron-wise weights from an energy function without additional learned parameters. Our pooled-context gate applies a related energy rule to a mean of aligned feature maps; the dilated-context gate computes the rule from learned contextual features and combines it with channel weights. These are adaptations of existing ideas. Their placement, value path and computational cost are the relevant architectural details, and their individual effects require controlled comparisons. DMF-Net pairs multi-branch detail extraction with dual-attention fusion [9]; TF-DEIM-DFINE combines a Mamba backbone, attention and a feature pyramid [10].
B014: 3. Proposed Method
B015: 3.1 Overall Architecture
B016: MPSMA-DEIM uses an HGNetv2-B0 backbone, a hybrid feature encoder and a D-FINE decoder trained within the DEIM framework [4,5]. Figure 1 distinguishes the two reported scales. Both use the same B0 stage widths and the same modified backbone. The n configuration supplies stride-16 and stride-32 features to an encoder of width 128, whereas the s configuration additionally supplies stride-8 features and uses width 256. Thus, the s result is a change in detector configuration, not evidence from a larger backbone alone. Within a given scale, MPS and MSCA are backbone modifications; the detection losses and decoder design are inherited.
B018: Fig. 1. Backbone and scale-dependent encoder inputs at 640 × 640. Tensor labels use channels × height × width. The dashed connection is present only in the s configuration.
B019: 3.2 Multi-branch Progressive Stem
B020: MPS replaces the standard stem with three paths (Fig. 2). In B0, the detail path uses two stride-one 3 × 3 convolution blocks with 8 channels, then 2 × 2 average pooling. The main path uses two 16-channel 3 × 3 blocks with strides two and one. The high-frequency path forms X − AvgPool3×3(X), using stride one and padding one, then applies a 1 × 1 projection and a depthwise 3 × 3 convolution with 4 channels. Bilinear interpolation with align_corners=False aligns this path to the main path. Odd input height or width prevents the branch outputs from being concatenated. Concatenating the three outputs produces a 28-channel feature Z at half the input resolution.
B021: A learned spatial gate maps the concatenation from 28 to 2 channels with a 1 × 1 convolution, batch normalization and the configured activation, then to one channel with a 7 × 7 convolution and sigmoid. The weighted feature is Z · [1 + A(Z)], where · denotes element-wise multiplication throughout. Fusion applies 1 × 1 and 3 × 3 convolution blocks with channel transitions 28 → 32 → 16, followed by a stride-two 3 × 3 block with 16 channels. The output stride is four, as in the original stem. The reported configurations enable the learnable affine block (LAB): each ConvBNAct in the detail, main, fusion and final downsampling paths applies a learned scalar scale and bias after activation. The high-frequency and spatial-gate paths do not contain this block. The name progressive refers to the two downsampling steps; it does not imply that the output has a higher spatial resolution than the baseline.
B023: Fig. 2. MPS for HGNetv2-B0 with H = W = 640 for the reported inputs. The three branches are concatenated, followed by residual weighting, fusion convolutions and a final stride-two convolution. BN denotes batch normalization.
B024: 3.3 Multi-scale Context-aware Attention
B025: The four stages use ESE, SimAM, MSCA-Lite and MSCA-Dilated (Fig. 3). Each HG block concatenates its features, projects them directly to its output width C using one 1 × 1 ConvBNAct, and then applies its gate. The local baseline uses two 1 × 1 ConvBNAct blocks with intermediate width C/2; the change therefore includes the projection path. B0 has one block in stages 1, 2 and 4 and two in stage 3, giving two MSCA-Lite instances. Stage outputs are 64, 256, 512 and 1024 channels at strides 4, 8, 16 and 32. Stage-2 SimAM uses variance denominator HW − 1 [8]; the two MSCA variants use denominator HW, so we describe them as SimAM-style gates.
B027: Fig. 3. Stage-specific gates within the B0 backbone. Each HG block aggregates concatenated features with a full-width 1 × 1 ConvBNAct followed by its gate.
B028: MSCA-Lite (Fig. 4) takes a feature map F with C channels and spatial size H × W. Let Pk denote k × k average pooling with stride k and Uk denote bilinear interpolation back to H × W with align_corners=False. The contextual feature is
B029: Fms = [F + U₂(P₂(F)) + U₄(P₄(F))] / 3.                                      (1)
B030: For each channel of a feature map T, define μ(T) as its spatial mean and v(T) as the spatial mean of [T − μ(T)]². With λ = 10⁻⁴, define the gate G and the MSCA-Lite output as
B031: G(T) = sigmoid{[T − μ(T)]² / [4(v(T) + λ)] + 0.5},   Ylite = F · G(Fms).    (2)
B032: The statistics are computed over spatial positions without collapsing the feature map before gating. G(Fms) has shape C × H × W and weights the original feature F. MSCA-Lite adds no learned parameters, but its pooling, interpolation and element-wise operations still incur computation. Its benefit for detection is a hypothesis to be separated from the other configuration changes.
B034: Fig. 4. MSCA-Lite. The energy gate is computed on the aligned contextual feature, and the value path is the original input F.
B035: MSCA-Dilated (Fig. 5) uses three branches Bd with dilation d ∈ {1, 2, 4}. Each branch applies a 1 × 1 convolution from C to C/8 channels, batch normalization and a rectified linear unit (ReLU), followed by a grouped 3 × 3 convolution from C/8 to C channels and batch normalization. The group count is C/8, so each input group produces eight output channels. This is a channel-multiplying grouped convolution, not a channel-preserving depthwise convolution. Concatenation and a learned 1 × 1 projection form the contextual feature; its gate uses the same G as Eq. (2):
B036: Cctx = Conv₁×₁(Concat[B₁(F), B₂(F), B₄(F)]),   As = G(Cctx).                 (3)
B037: A separate channel gate uses global average pooling (GAP) of F and two 1 × 1 convolutions, reducing C to C/8 and restoring C. Their weights and biases are W₁, W₂ and b₁, b₂, respectively:
B038: Ac = sigmoid{W₂ ReLU[W₁ GAP(F) + b₁] + b₂}.                               (4)
B039: The output weights the original input by both gates, with the channel gate broadcast over spatial positions:
B040: Ydilated = F · As · Ac.                                                     (5)
B041: For kernel size k and dilation d, the effective kernel size is
B042: keff = k + (k − 1)(d − 1).                                                  (6)
B043: With k = 3, the effective sizes are 3, 5 and 9 for d = 1, 2 and 4. All branches use stride one and padding d, preserving H × W. For stage 4, C = 1024 and the reduced width and group count are both 128. The contextual spatial gate remains channel-specific, with shape C × H × W; it is not a single H × W mask.
B045: Fig. 5. MSCA-Dilated. The contextual and channel gates both modulate F. The reduction ratio is 8 and the dilation rates are 1, 2 and 4.
B046: 3.4 Backbone Activation
B047: The full configuration selects SiLU, f(x) = x sigmoid(x) [11], for the configurable convolution blocks in the stem and backbone stages. This does not replace every activation: the internal branch and channel-gate activations of MSCA-Dilated remain ReLU. The encoder and decoder keep their configured activations. SiLU is a configuration choice rather than a novel component, and the historical runs do not isolate its effect because the nominal SiLU variant also changes feature aggregation and batch size.
B048: 4. Experiments
B049: 4.1 Dataset and Implementation Details
B050: The experiments use the VisDrone2019 detection data [2], with 6,471 training images and 548 validation images covering ten categories. Validation results are reported separately from test-dev results. Figure 6 shows two identified validation images. The local configurations load COCO-format annotations and resize inputs to 640 × 640, with 300 postprocessor predictions per image across categories. We report COCO-style average precision (AP) and average recall (AR) [12]. The retained evaluator files specify ten intersection-over-union (IoU) thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05, 101 recall thresholds, ten categories, class-aware matching (useCats=1) and maxDets=[1, 10, 100] per image and category.
B051: The retained annotation files contain 343,205 training boxes and 38,759 validation boxes. All have iscrowd=0 and no ignore field. The original validation annotations additionally contain 1,378 ignored-region entries and 32 entries labelled others, which are absent from the converted file. The official toolkit filters detections using ignored regions and reports AP with a limit of 500 detections [13]. Thus, deleting those entries alone does not reproduce the official protocol. The local metrics are kept separate from challenge rankings, and confirming that these retained files are identical to those used in each historical run remains necessary.

B053: Fig. 6. VisDrone2019 validation inputs [2]: 0000001_02999_d_0000005.jpg (left) and 0000280_02201_d_0000623.jpg (right). These are dataset examples, without detection overlays.
B054: The six recorded runs use seed 0 and contain 320 logged validation epochs, indexed 0–319. Their configurations disable pretrained-backbone loading and tuning; the nominal SiLU variant also records a resume path to its own last checkpoint. AdamW has betas (0.9, 0.999), default decay 10⁻⁴ and name-based exemptions. The n rates are 4 × 10⁻⁴ for backbone names and 8 × 10⁻⁴ otherwise. In the inspected code, s uses 2 × 10⁻⁴ for backbone names without bn and 4 × 10⁻⁴ for other names; some BatchNorm parameters consequently receive the lower rate and default decay. Most importantly, the historical n baseline uses training and validation batches of 16, whereas the other runs use batches of 4. Equal epoch counts therefore do not imply an equal number of optimizer updates.
B055: The saved augmentation policy has boundaries [4, 156, 296], using zero-based epoch indices. In the inspected implementation, the Mosaic selector and batch MixUp each have probability 0.5 only at epochs 4–155. Photometric distortion, zoom-out and IoU cropping are eligible at epochs 4–295; Mosaic-selected samples skip zoom-out and IoU cropping. These strong augmentations are off from epoch 296; horizontal flipping and 640 × 640 resizing remain. Batch multi-scale resizing is disabled and exponential moving average (EMA) is enabled. Tables 2 and 3 retain each run’s complete highest-AP log row. AP averages ten IoU thresholds; AP50 and AP75 use 0.50 and 0.75. APs uses original-image area [0, 1024] square pixels; AR@100 denotes recall at maxDets=100 in this class-aware evaluation. These validation-selected, single-seed results provide no independent test estimate or uncertainty across runs.

B066: Table 3 reports the complete n and s configurations on the validation split. Both use HGNetv2-B0, but the s configuration adds a stride-8 encoder input, increases the encoder and decoder width from 128 to 256, changes the decoder sampling-point allocation from [6, 6] to [3, 6, 3], and uses a different learning-rate configuration. Its higher AP therefore describes this combined scale-and-training change, rather than an isolated benefit of the proposed backbone modules.

B079: 4.5 Re-evaluation of Retained Checkpoints
B080: We re-evaluated the retained N0, N4 and S1 EMA checkpoints once on all 548 validation images using the archived current implementation. The checks used FP32 on an AMD Ryzen 7 9700X CPU, PyTorch 2.6.0, TorchVision 0.21.0 and faster-coco-eval 1.6.5. The original validation batch sizes were retained (16 for N0 and 4 for N4/S1), together with the 640 × 640 transforms, postprocessor and local COCO-style settings of Section 4.1. Each checkpoint and postprocessor loaded with strict key and shape checks; no missing or unexpected keys were found. The models were evaluated without deployment conversion. The verification records bind the predictions and metrics to SHA-256 hashes of the weights, configuration, current source, annotations and validation images. The historical runtime versions remain unverified.

B084: 4.6 Late-epoch Variation and Evidence Limits
B085: For the s run, the last 20 logged validation AP values have a mean of 0.287065, a sample standard deviation of 0.000306 and a range of 0.001113. The best logged epoch is 302, and the final logged epoch is 319. These statistics summarize within-run variation only; they do not measure reproducibility across seeds or rule out validation-selection effects.
B086: The available evidence supports a documented architectural design and single-run configuration results. It does not yet isolate shallow-detail preservation, contextual weighting or activation effects. Some retained checkpoints contain last_epoch metadata that differs from the best-AP log index. The inspected solver selects a stage-one checkpoint by validation AP and may restore it during later training, which can reset last_epoch; whether this solver was used historically is unverified. This field alone cannot identify the evaluation that produced a historical value. Section 4.5 establishes current checkpoint-to-evaluation records for N0, N4 and S1, while exact historical attribution still requires the matching training-code, data and environment records. Qualitative box overlays without matched thresholds and ground truth are insufficient to resolve these limitations.
B087: 5. Conclusion
B088: MPSMA-DEIM combines a three-path shallow stem with stage-specific contextual attention in a DEIM-D-FINE detector. The implementation uses residual stem weighting, parameter-free pooled-context gates and a learned dilated-context gate, while retaining the host encoder and decoder design for each scale. The recorded n and s configurations reach validation AP of 0.2270 and 0.2876, respectively. Separate CPU checks of N0, N4 and S1 strictly load the retained EMA weights and yield AP differences of at most 0.00147 pp from the historical best-log values, with remaining differences in other metrics. These observations are promising enough to motivate controlled evaluation, but the historical baseline differs in batch size and aggregation structure, preventing isolated component attribution. The archived timing results describe GPU network-plus-postprocessor execution and do not establish an onboard deployment guarantee. The next empirical requirement is a matched baseline/full-model comparison with fixed data, initialization policy, training budget and evaluation, followed by targeted component controls if individual contribution claims are retained.
