Image Acquisition and Preprocessing
An overview of the dataset is shown in Fig. 1.T h er a w
frames are captured from the side of a building with a view-
ing angle slightly less than perpendicular to the street plane.
We apply a perspective transformation so that the street plane
is viewed at 90
◦, giving a true bird’s-eye view. This transfor-
mation takes 0 .203 ms on a Jetson Orin Nano and 0 .035 ms
on an A100 GPU, and is therefore negligible in practice. The
original images are captured at a resolution of 1920 × 1080
and subsequently transformed to obtain a top-down view and
cropped to square images. Following the input size scaling
rules for YOLO models and to enable low processing times
without relying on sliced inference techniques (Akyon et al.,
2022), the input image size is chosen as 832 × 832, with the
intersection centered in the frame.
The dataset contains data collected at all times of day
(Fig. 1a–d), which results in a large variability in lighting
conditions as sharp shadows are cast by buildings over the
intersection depending on the time of day. These shadows
result in large brightness changes that object detection mod-
els must handle (for example, the scenes shown in Fig. 1a
and g). The background is highly variable due to changes in
shadows and lighting, frequent changes in the scene back-
ground, birds ﬂying over the intersection, parked cars, and
waiting pedestrians. The foreground is also difﬁcult to esti-
mate given the small size of the shadows and their similarity
to pedestrians wearing dark colors. The frames cover all sea-
sons of the year and weather conditions that include (i) sunny,
(ii) overcast, and (iii) rain.
Annotations
For all frames, axis-aligned bounding boxes are provided
for pedestrian and vehicle classes. All annotations were per-
formed in three rounds: ﬁrst, an initial annotator labeled the
frames; second, a different annotator reviewed these labels;
and ﬁnally, all frames were reviewed by a third annotator.
Constellation features many challenging frames with large
crowds where separating individuals is difﬁcult; some exam-
ples of such scenes are shown in Fig. 3, where annotation
requires careful reasoning by human annotators.

Train/Test Splits
The dataset comprises 13 ,314 annotated frames, divided
into 10,230 training images and 3 ,084 test images collected
across 28 time intervals. Eighteen of these time intervals are
obtained from videos captured in 2019 and 2020: ﬁve con-
tain 300 frames, and the remaining 13 contain 450 frames,
for a total of 7 ,350 annotated frames. The dataset also con-
tains 5,964 annotated frames from 10 different scenes during
2023, increasing the variability in subject, lighting, weather,
time of year, and background conditions. We show successive
samples of frames from the dataset in Fig. 2.
Training Set The training set consists of 10 ,230 frames
divided into 19 subsets collected on different days with differ-
ent weather and time-of-day conditions. Fifteen of the subsets
are from 2019 and 2020 (corresponding to 6 ,160 frames),
and four are from 2023 (corresponding to 4,070 frames). All
frames were collected from an intersection in Manhattan.
Test Set The test set consists of 3,084 frames divided into
9 subsets collected on different days with different weather
and time-of-day conditions. This test set contains 12 ,399
vehicle and 14,229 pedestrian bounding boxes. Three of the
subsets are from 2019 and 2020 (corresponding to 1 ,201
frames), and six are from 2023; ﬁve of the 2023 subsets fea-
ture the intersection before repavement. Each subset contains
between 70 and 2,000 frames. No time interval appears in
both the train and test sets.
Performance Drift We provide a separate performance
drift dataset with the acquisition year marked for studies
involving the transfer of knowledge from older frames to
newer frames. For this, we collected 4,731 images from 2020
and 4,716 frames from 2023, covering footage from 26 weeks
per year, taken at the same time of day.
Weather-Speciﬁc Test Scenarios We tested our best-
performing model on a set of specialized test sets that only
include frames corresponding to speciﬁc extreme conditions,
including (i) heavy rain, (ii) heavy fog, and (iii) heavy snow.

4.7 Semi-Supervised Object Detection
To improve the performance of the models, we deployed the
Constellation-trained YOLOv8x model in inference mode
for real-time data tracking and bounding box collection as
an augmentation approach. Using a tracking-by-detection
paradigm (Bewley et al.,2016) helps smooth predictions over
time and reduce false positives and false negatives. Another
signiﬁcant challenge when using such a model for auto-
matic data collection is dealing with objects that have not
been detected. To reduce this false negative rate in the data
collected in this manner, we masked all areas of the image
beyond the bounding box bounds with a median image cal-
culated over the last 120 s of the video stream.
The unlabeled data for semi-supervision was collected
over 4 days, with one frame being saved every 20 s. We
refer to this dataset as BoxMask. We used this dataset for
a 125-epoch pretraining step, followed by 125-epoch train-
ing on human-annotated Constellation images. The results
are presented in Table 11. We ﬁnd that this dataset achieves
a similar performance to VisDrone, suggesting a pretraining
stage with more diverse pseudo-labeled data could be used
as a replacement for standard pretraining datasets.
4.8 Performance Drift
We explored the effect of removing portions of the training
data based on the year of acquisition to look at how changes
in street-level conditions affect models over time. We ran two
experiments (i) using the data from 2020 only, (ii) using the
data from 2023 only. To get enough data to form comparably
sized training and testing splits, we created two new datasets
using historical footage, covering at least 50 separate captures
throughout the year and used 25% of the data for testing, mak-
ing sure that no frame from the same week appears in both the
training and testing sets. YOLOv8x models are trained with
our hyperparameter and augmentation recipe for 125 epochs
on both datasets and each model is tested on both splits. We
summarize the experiments in Table 12. We ﬁnd that mod-
els trained only on 2020 data achieve 96.8 mAP@0.5 on the
2020 split but drop to 89.7 mAP@0.5 on the 2023 split, while
models trained only on 2023 data show the opposite pattern.
This asymmetry underscores the need for continuous data
refresh in long-term deployments.
We thus postulate that, for deploying object detection
models, regularly updated data collection and reﬁnement
need to be a standard procedure. Future research could focus
on minimizing the performance difference between the splits
(i) and (ii) through novel training or augmentation strategies.
5 Conclusion
We introduced the Constellation dataset, consisting of 13K
images of trafﬁc intersection scenes acquired by a high-
elevation camera deployed in a large metropolis. The dataset
is designed for training and evaluating AI models targeted for
large-scale smart city deployment, where cameras and other
sensors are co-located with embedded computing devices
with limited compute and memory. Our analysis of state-
of-the-art object detection models in this dataset highlights
