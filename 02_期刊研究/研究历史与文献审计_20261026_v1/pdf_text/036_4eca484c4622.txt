Abstract 
Aerial scenes exhibit a mismatch between where computation is spent and where infor-
mation resides: targets occupy a small, unevenly distributed fraction of the frame, yet de-
tectors process every region at identical resolution. Tiling and clustering pipelines exploit 
this sparsity, but the rule deciding which regions deserve magnification is hand-designed 
and never observes the detection loss. AFWD-Net removes that separation. A coarse den-
sity estimator, run on a heavily downsampled copy of the frame at negligible cost, pro-
duces a spatial prior over likely target locations; Gumbel top -K relaxation and a spatial-
transformer sampler convert this prior into a small set of high -resolution crops whose 
centers and scales are differentiable, so the gradient that measures detection quality also 
determines where resolution is allocated. Within each crop, a l earnable wavelet decom-
position separates approximation from directional detail sub-bands and enhances the lat-
ter under edge supervision confined to annotated regions, restoring the high-frequency 
structure that stride downsampling erases, precisely the cue separating a genuine sub-32-
pixel target from background clutter. Detection queries are seeded from the same density 
field rather than a fixed lattice, and crop-level predictions are merged by density -cali-
brated soft suppression. On VisDrone2019-DET the model attains 49.3% mAP@0.5, 30.4% 
mAP@[0.5:0.95] and 22.8% APS at 15.7 M parameters; a distilled 7.2 M student remains 
competitive with all compared methods. Experiments on UAVDT, AI -TOD-v2 and 
SODA-A assess cross-dataset generalization. 
Keywords: differentiable crop selection; learned resolution allocation; foveated object  
detection; discrete wavelet transform; Gumbel top-K sampling; density-conditioned  
queries; aerial imagery; edge deployment 
 
1. Introduction 
UAVs have become a routine sensing platform for urban traffic monitoring, precision 
agriculture, infrastructure inspection, environmental surveying, and search and rescue 
operations [1]. In nearly all of these applications the objects of interest —vehicles, people, 
livestock, defects, and debris appear small relative to the frame because they are imaged 
from altitude and at oblique angles. A large fraction of these targets falls within what the 
literature defines as the small and tiny object regime: objects occupying fewer than 32 × 32 
pixels, less than roughly one percent of the total image area. At these scales the accuracy 
of otherwise strong detectors degrades sharply, and closing this gap is widely regarded 
as one of the defining challenges of a erial computer vision [2]. Here are three difficulties 
that are compounded in the UAV setting. First, limited pixel coverage yields impover-
ished feature representations a target only a handful of pixels across simply does not con-
tain enough signal for a deep network to form a discriminative descriptor [3]. Second, the 
successive stride convolutions and pooling layers that give modern backbones their large 
receptive fields also progressively destroy the fine, high-frequency detail on which small-
object recognition depends, so that by the deeper stages of the network a tiny object may 
have no distinct spatial footprint at all [4]. Third, objects in aerial scenes are distributed 
unevenly; they cluster densely in some regions and are absent from large background 
areas, and occlusion within these clusters tends to be locally concentrated rather than ran-
domly scattered, producing feature confusion between neighboring instances [5]. The 
value of a UAV detector is realized only when it runs onboard, in real time, on an embed-
ded accelerator with a fixed power and memory envelope [6]. This rules out the brute
