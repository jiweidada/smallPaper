Enhanced YOLOv11n for small 
object detection in UAV imagery: 
higher accuracy with fewer 
parameters
Hongkai Zhu1 & Xianghua Xie2
Object detection in UAV imagery faces several challenges due to high-altitude aerial capture: targets 
are densely distributed, small objects account for a large proportion, and onboard computing 
power is limited, leading to low detection accuracy and high rates of false and missed detections. To 
address these issues, this paper proposes an improved YOLOv11 model. First, we design a Multiscale 
Edge-Feature Adaptive Selection (MSEAF) module in the backbone to effectively cope with the 
predominance of small objects and weak edge information. Second, we use the ScalCat and Scal3DC 
modules to reconstruct the neck and add a P2 small-object detection head, alleviating feature 
degradation in multiscale processing and improving the utilization of high-resolution information. 
Finally, we design a shared, reparameterized lightweight detection head (SRepD) to resolve the 
computational redundancy and insufficient feature fusion of conventional heads. On the VisDrone2019 
dataset, compared with the YOLOv11n baseline, our model increases mAP50 and Precision by 4.6% 
while reducing the number of parameters by approximately 8.5%. On the TinyPerson dataset, which 
contains only two extremely small categories, our model improves mAP50 and Precision by 5.5% 
and 5.6%, respectively, with a 7.7% reduction in parameters relative to YOLOv11n. Compared with 
the larger YOLOv11s, our model achieves gains of 3.8% in mAP50 and 3.2% in Precision while using 
only 25% of its parameters, demonstrating cross-scale performance superiority. On the HazyDet 
dataset, the first UAV-perspective hazy scene dataset, our model also demonstrates superior detection 
performance compared to the YOLOv11n baseline.
Over the past decade, unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) technology has developed rapidly and has become an 
indispensable tool in modern society. According to a market research report1, the global UAV market is expected 
to grow from USD 36.4 billion in 2024 to USD 125.9 billion by 2032, with a compound annual growth rate 
of 17.3%. UAVs show application potential in both military and civilian domains. Civilian scenarios already 
cover aerial surveillance, parcel delivery, precision agriculture, intelligent transportation (including traffic 
monitoring), search and rescue, post-disaster and wildfire management, and remote sensing 2. When equipped 
with imaging payloads, UAVs can conduct real-time monitoring over large areas and accurately capture key 
indicators such as crowd density in critical regions and road traffic flow, thereby providing technical support 
for public safety assurance 3. However, the typical operating altitude of UAVs–generally between 100 and 500 
m–has a marked impact on imagery. In the collected images, approximately 60–80% of targets are smaller than 
32 × 32 pixels, and more than 70% are even smaller than 16 pixels4–6. Such small targets commonly suffer from 
scarce feature information, low contrast, and a limited number of effective keypoints. Because the pixel scale is 
extremely small, visual details are heavily compressed and it is difficult to stand out from complex backgrounds, 
making it hard for models to capture sufficient discriminative cues and increasing the difficulty of accurate 
localization and classification. During flight, airflow-induced, high-frequency vibrations, abrupt illumination 
changes between day and night, and environmental interference from diverse ground objects further exacerbate 
the detection challenge. Lightweight models deliver fast inference. However, when handling extremely small 
objects, their limited capacity hinders the retention of shallow fine-grained details and the extraction of deep 
semantic features, leaving substantial room for improving detection accuracy.

first achieving great success in natural-language processing, its outstanding performance in NLP motivated 
researchers to apply Transformers to computer vision, especially object detection. DETR 15 was the first to 
introduce the Transformer into end-to-end object detection, eliminating region proposals and non-maximum 
suppression (NMS), but it suffers from slow convergence, low inference speed, and poor accuracy on small 
objects. RT-DETR16 mitigates these drawbacks through a CNN–Transformer hybrid architecture, IoU-aware 
query selection, and an efficient hybrid encoder, improving convergence and inference efficiency; nevertheless, 
it still shows strong hardware dependence and a large parameter count, which restricts its use in lightweight 
and real-time scenarios.CNN-based object-detection methods can be further divided into two-stage and one-
stage approaches. Two-stage methods first generate candidate regions and then perform classification and 
bounding-box regression. R-CNN17, the pioneering two-stage method, was the first to successfully apply deep 
learning to object detection. Its pipeline uses Selective Search to propose regions, extracts features for each 
region with a CNN, and finally applies an SVM classifier together with a bounding-box regressor to complete 
detection. Although R-CNN achieved a breakthrough in accuracy, it suffers from slow detection speed, complex 
training, and heavy storage overhead. To address these issues, Faster R-CNN 18 replaces hand-crafted proposal 
generation with a region-proposal network (RPN) and shares features to enable end-to-end training, improving 
efficiency while maintaining accuracy. Overall, two-stage methods such as R-CNN and Faster R-CNN deliver 
high accuracy and are easy to extend to multitask settings, but processing many proposals leads to slow inference 
and high computational complexity. Consequently, for UAV imagery it is difficult for these approaches to 
simultaneously meet the requirements of accuracy, real-time performance, and lightweight deployment.By 
contrast, one-stage object-detection algorithms make a single prediction of location and category directly on 
feature maps at preset anchors or object centers. They offer fast inference but typically sacrifice some accuracy. 
Representative one-stage methods include SSD, RetinaNet, and the YOLO family. SSD 19 extracts feature maps 
from different network layers and realizes multi-scale detection by detecting small objects on shallow layers 
and large objects on deeper layers. It uses predefined default boxes as prior knowledge and predicts, at each 
feature-map location, multiple bounding boxes of different sizes and aspect ratios. This design allows SSD to 
retain relatively fast inference while handling objects of different scales, making it a milestone among one-stage 
detectors. However, SSD performs poorly on small objects; its default-box hyperparameters require careful 
manual design; and shallow-layer features offer limited semantic information. RetinaNet 20 introduces Focal 
Loss to address the severe class-imbalance problem: training in traditional one-stage detectors is dominated by 
many easy negatives, which hampers learning on hard examples. By dynamically reweighting the loss, Focal Loss 
reduces the contribution of easy negatives and focuses the model on hard samples. RetinaNet also uses an FPN to 
fuse multi-scale features and was the first to show that a one-stage detector can match the accuracy of two-stage 
methods. Y et the FPN increases model complexity and computational cost, resulting in relatively slow inference 
and limiting its suitability where strict real-time performance is required.
The YOLO series began in 2016 with YOLOv1 21 by Redmon et al., which reformulated object detection as 
a single regression problem that predicts an object’s location and category in one forward pass, fundamentally 
changing the traditional detection paradigm. Subsequent versions–YOLOv2 22 , YOLOv3 23 , and YOLOv4 24 
–continued to refine the architecture and algorithms; while preserving the advantages of fast detection and 
lightweight design, they gradually approached the accuracy of two-stage methods 25. Mature versions in recent 
years include YOLOv526, YOLOv87 , and YOLOv1111 . They all comprise three components: a backbone for feature 
extraction, a neck for multiscale feature fusion, and a detection head that outputs the final predictions, delivering 
strong performance in real-time applications. Among these, YOLOv8–released in 2023 as a mainstream version–
introduced the C2f module and a decoupled detection head, achieving a notable balance between speed and 
accuracy. For aerial small-object detection, many researchers have improved upon YOLOv8. For example, FBRT-
YOLO8, ESOD-YOLO9, and GAM-YOLOv810 are listed in Table 1. Taking FBRT-YOLO[22] as an example, the 
FCM module integrates shallow and deep feature information, and MKP replaces the downsampling operation 
to simplify the network structure while removing the P5 detection head. Using YOLOv8n as the baseline on the 
VisDrone2019 dataset, the parameter count is reduced by 72%, and the small-object detection performance is 
slightly improved. On the basis of YOLOv8, YOLOv11 was further optimized: first, the C3K2 module replaces 
the C2f module, using double small convolutions instead of large convolutions to improve feature aggregation 
efficiency; second, the C2PSA mechanism is introduced to enhance spatial attention, improving small-object 
detection in complex scenes; third, the detection head uses deep convolutions to reduce computational load, 
achieving a balance between speed and accuracy. These improvements make YOLOv11 significantly reduce 
the model’s parameter count while maintaining detection accuracy, making it more suitable as a baseline for 
research improvements. Researchers have made improvements to YOLOv11, such as HFC-YOLOv11 12 and
