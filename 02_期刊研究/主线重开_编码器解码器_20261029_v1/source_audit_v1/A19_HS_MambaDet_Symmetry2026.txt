Small-Object Detection in Foggy Scenes via High-Frequency-Enhanced
Feature Fusion and Compact State-Space Modeling
Yue Chen, Feng Xu * and Jinghao Song

                                         Abstract
                                         In adverse weather and rapidly changing scenes, fog severely reduces image contrast and
                                         obscures target textures, causing small-object detection to suffer from feature weakening
                                         and background interference. Many existing detectors, meanwhile, rely on computation-
                                         ally intensive feature modeling, making it difficult to achieve real-time inference while
                                         effectively mitigating fog-induced degradation. To address these challenges, we propose
                                         HS-MambaDet, a frequency-compensated hidden-state state-space detection network for
                                         accurate and efficient small-object detection in foggy environments. Specifically, we embed
                                         a lightweight SSD-based state-space modeling module with frequency-domain window
                                         attention (FWS-SSD) into the backbone, preserving long-range dependency modeling with
                                         low computational overhead while emphasizing informative high-frequency details and
                                         attenuating low-frequency haze interference. This study highlights a symmetry-inspired
                                         balance between global context modeling and local detail restoration. In the neck network,
                                         a multi-scale frequency-spatial fusion (MFSF) module further strengthens fine-grained
                                         object representations and cross-scale contextual interactions. In addition, we introduce
                                         a fog-aware detection loss to better supervise low-contrast and detail-deficient regions,
                                         improving detection robustness in foggy scenes. Extensive experiments on RTTS and
                                         Cityscapes demonstrate clear and consistent gains: HS-MambaDet outperforms represen-
                                         tative one-stage, two-stage, and state-space-based detectors by up to 4.3% in mAP@0.5
                                         and 6.5% in mAP@0.5:0.95, while maintaining competitive inference efficiency, thereby
                                         achieving a favorable accuracy-efficiency trade-off for foggy small-object detection.

                                         Keywords: object detection; Mamba; high-frequency perception; fusion hidden state
                                         compression

                         2. Related Work
                               Over the past decade, object detection has evolved from proposal-driven pipelines to
                         end-to-end single-stage frameworks, and has recently expanded toward hybrid architec-
                         tures that incorporate state space modeling. For small-object detection in adverse weather
                         conditions such as fog, existing approaches can be broadly categorized into two-stage
                         detectors, single-stage detectors typified by the YOLO series, and emerging detectors
                         that integrate state space models into the detection pipeline. In parallel, fog-oriented
                         enhancement and preprocessing techniques have become an active research direction,
                         aiming to alleviate visibility degradation before or during detection. The following review
                         summarizes these lines of research, highlighting their relevance to this study and their
                         remaining limitations.
                               Two-stage detectors, represented by Faster R-CNN [20], first generate candidate re-
                         gions using a Region Proposal Network and then perform classification and bounding
                         box regression in a second stage. These methods are often treated as accuracy-oriented
                         baselines due to their proposal refinement and feature sharing. Subsequent develop-
                         ments have further enhanced two-stage performance. For example, the Feature Pyramid
                         Network (FPN) [21,22] improves multi-scale representation through a top-down pyramid,
                         and Cascade R-CNN [23] increases detection quality by progressively raising intersection
                         over union thresholds across stages. Although two-stage methods can achieve strong
                         performance in complex scenes, their multi-stage inference introduces latency and compu-
                         tational cost, limiting real-time deployment. Moreover, these methods typically assume
                         relatively clear textures and sufficient contrast. Under fog-induced contrast reduction and
                         high-frequency detail loss, both proposal generation and downstream discrimination can
                         degrade, reducing the robustness of small-object detection.
                               The YOLO family is widely used in real-time settings because it completes detection
                         in a single forward pass. Early versions such as YOLOv3 and YOLOv4 improved the



                                                                                    https://doi.org/10.3390/sym18020320
Symmetry 2026, 18, 320                                                                                            4 of 28



                         speed-accuracy balance through stronger backbones and multi-scale detection heads [24].
                         More recent implementations, such as YOLOv5 [25] and YOLOv8 [26], have been adopted
                         in a wide range of engineering applications. Prior studies indicate that YOLO is advanta-
                         geous for low-latency and resource-limited deployment, with improvements in pyramid
                         fusion and attention design enhancing small-object performance in clear conditions. How-
                         ever, as a regression-oriented single-stage framework, YOLO often relies on local textures
                         and edge cues, which are weakened by fog, thereby reducing localization and classifica-
                         tion reliability. Additionally, standard multi-scale fusion can become ambiguous under
                         low-contrast conditions, hindering the detection of distant and tiny objects. Therefore, prac-
                         tical foggy-scene deployment often requires dedicated robustness enhancements beyond
                         standard YOLO improvements.
                               State space models and the recently proposed Mamba framework [17] provide an
                         alternative to self-attention through linear-complexity sequence scanning. Mamba has
                         demonstrated high throughput and strong long-sequence modeling capabilities in language,
                         audio, and selected vision tasks, making it a promising mechanism for combining efficiency
                         with long-range dependency modeling. Motivated by these advantages, recent studies have
                         integrated Mamba or state space modules into detection backbones or replaced attention
                         blocks with state space layers, forming hybrid designs such as Mamba-YOLO and other
                         state space-based variants [27]. These detectors aim to strengthen global context reasoning
                         while preserving real-time inference, improving discrimination under occlusion and clutter.
                         Nevertheless, in foggy scenes, existing state space-based detectors still face challenges
                         due to severe attenuation of high-frequency details. The smoothing behavior of state
                         propagation further weakens small-object signals during hidden-state updates, and state
                         space modeling alone does not explicitly compensate for high-frequency loss or address
                         low-contrast regions. This motivates the integration of frequency-aware enhancement
                         mechanisms to complement efficient global modeling.
                               Methods that mitigate fog degradation can be grouped into two main directions.
                         The first direction performs image restoration or dehazing as preprocessing, including
                         end-to-end dehazing networks such as DehazeNet [28] and AOD-Net [29]. The second
                         direction introduces dehazing principles or fog-aware objectives directly into detectors to
                         support end-to-end robustness [3]. Preprocessing-based dehazing can recover part of the
                         missing high-frequency information at the input stage, which may benefit downstream
                         detection. However, standalone restoration networks usually introduce additional com-
                         putational cost and may suffer from domain shift when deployed across different fog
                         conditions. For small-object detection, related work has emphasized improved multi-scale
                         fusion, channel attention, spatial attention, and super-resolution enhancement, such as
                         CE-FPN [30] and super-resolution assisted detection. These methods help preserve resolu-
                         tion and amplify small-object cues. However, in real foggy scenes, they typically still need
                         to be combined with strategies that explicitly address low contrast and frequency-domain
                         noise. Overall, improving small-object detection in foggy scenes requires a coordinated
                         design that combines efficient global modeling, local enhancement for high-frequency
                         detail recovery, and fog-aware detection objectives [31].
                               Based on the above discussion, existing methods have not yet achieved a satisfac-
                         tory balance among real-time efficiency, global modeling capability, and high-frequency
                         detail preservation. Two-stage detectors can deliver strong accuracy but at substantial
                         computational cost. The YOLO family is efficient but remains sensitive to the weakening
                         of edge and texture cues in fog. Mamba and state space modeling provide efficient global
                         reasoning but are comparatively insensitive to fine high-frequency details. The proposed
                         HS-MambaDet addresses these gaps by combining a hidden-state-compressed lightweight
                         state space backbone for efficient global modeling with frequency-windowed attention and



                                                                                     https://doi.org/10.3390/sym18020320
Symmetry 2026, 18, 320                                                                                           5 of 28



                         high-frequency-aware enhancement to recover fine details in foggy scenes. In addition,
                         a fog-aware detection loss is employed to adaptively constrain low-contrast regions. To-
                         gether, these components yield an improved accuracy-efficiency trade-off for small-object
                         detection in foggy environments.

                         3. HS-MambaDet Method
                         3.1. Overall Framework
                              In this section, we introduce the overall architecture of the proposed HS-MambaDet.
                         As shown in Figure 1, HS-MambaDet consists of three main components: the backbone
                         network, the neck network, and the detection head. The backbone employs a four-stage
                         hierarchical structure jointly constructed by CNNs and FWS-SSD Blocks, where each stage’s
                         FWS-SSD Block outputs multi-scale features, denoted as C3, C4, C5, which are subsequently
                         fed into the neck network for feature fusion and downstream object detection. The detailed
                         structure of the FWS-SSD Block will be described in Section 3.2.




                         Figure 1. HS-MambaDet Model Block Diagram.

                              For a given input image of size H × W × 3, it is first projected into a C-dimensional
                         embedding space by a stem consisting of two consecutive 3 × 3 CNN layers. Subsequently,
                         features are rapidly extracted at the high input resolution and downsampled into overlap-
                         ping patches of size H/4 × W/4 × 3 using CNN-based feature extraction layers. To ensure
                         faster convergence and training stability, residual connections are also employed in this
                         process. This procedure can be expressed as:

                                                         z = BN(Conv3×3 (ẑ)) + z                                   (2)

                         Here, GELU and BN denote the Gaussian Error Linear Unit activation function and Batch
                         Normalization, respectively.
                               After preliminary feature extraction by the convolutional layers, the image features
                         are fed into the stage-wise feature extraction network composed of FWS-SSD Blocks, where

                         Here, zi and zi+1 denote the image features from the previous and the subsequent

                              After multi-stage and multi-level feature extraction by the backbone network, the
                         resulting multi-scale features {C3, C4, C5} are fed into the neck network for further fusion.
                         As shown in Figure 1, the neck network adopts a top-down multi-scale feature fusion
                         structure. At each scale, the current layer feature Ci from the backbone is concatenated
                         with the upsampled result Pi from the lower-level feature, and then enhanced and fused

                         multi-scale frequency-spatial fusion (MFSF) module performs collaborative modeling in
                         both the spatial and frequency domains to address the degradation characteristics of foggy
                         images. It effectively enhances fine-grained object information across different scales while
                         suppressing low-frequency noise introduced by fog. The resulting fused multi-scale fea-
                         tures f i are then fed into the corresponding detection heads to achieve precise detection of
                         objects at various scales.

                         3.2. FWS-SSD Block Based on Mamba and Frequency Domain Attention
                              In this section, we describe the FWS-SSD block, which serves as the fundamental
                         unit of the proposed backbone. As illustrated in Figure 2, FWS-SSD is a hybrid feature
                         modeling module that combines state space modeling with frequency-windowed attention.
                         The block follows a two-branch architecture that captures global dependencies and local
                         discriminative cues, while maintaining high throughput and low computational complexity.
                         Specifically, it contains a Mamba-inspired state space branch and a frequency-windowed
                         attention branch. The two branches are designed to be complementary. Such symmetry
                         arises from a dual view: global dependencies versus local high-frequency structures.
                         The state space branch provides efficient long-range dependency modeling, whereas the
                         attention branch enhances high-frequency detail responses, which are often weakened in
                         foggy scenes and not sufficiently emphasized by standard state space propagation.
                              We first present the Mamba state space branch. In this branch, we employ a lightweight
                         hidden-state module named HSM-SSD. The key motivation is to perform channel mixing
                         and gating in a low-dimensional latent hidden state, reducing computation while retaining
                         the ability to model long-sequence dependencies. As shown in Figure 3, HSM-SSD includes
                         two symmetric sub-branches. This symmetry forms a paired pathway for modeling and
                         compensation. One sub-branch performs SSD-based state space modeling, while the
                         other serves as a compensation path without SSD. The compensation path uses additional
                         convolution layers along with Sigmoid Linear Unit activations, introduced to compensate
                         for possible information loss caused by sequential constraints in SSD. The outputs of the
                         two sub-branches are concatenated along the channel dimension and then fused using a
                         linear projection layer. This operation produces an integrated representation that balances




                                                                                          https://doi.org/10.3390/sym18020320
Symmetry 2026, 18, 320                                                                                            7 of 28



                         sequential modeling capability with spatial feature expressiveness. The overall procedure
                         can be formulated as:

                                                    X1 = HSM − SSD(σ (Conv(Linear( Xin ))))                          (5)

                                                          X2 = σ (Conv(Linear( Xin )))                               (6)

                                                       Xmamba = Linear(Concat( X1 , X2 ))                            (7)

                         Here, Xin denotes the input to the HSM-SSD module, and Xout represents its final output.
                         This combination ensures that the resulting feature representation integrates both sequential
                         and spatial information, leveraging the advantages of both branches.

                               Furthermore, in foggy object detection, performance degradation is largely caused
                         by dense fog obscuring key visual cues and introducing strong background disturbance.
                         Existing studies and frequency-domain analyses indicate that fog effects are mainly man-
                         ifested as low-frequency components in feature representations, whereas object details
                         such as edges and textures are more concentrated in mid-frequency and high-frequency
                         bands. Therefore, purely spatial-domain feature modeling often struggles to simultaneously
                         suppress fog interference and retain fine object details.
                               To address this challenge, we adopt a frequency-domain viewpoint and design a
                         window-based frequency-domain attention module, termed FW-MSA, to compensate
                         for the limited ability of Mamba-style state space modeling to explicitly capture contex-
                         tual relationships. As shown in Figure 2, and following the window partition strategy
                         used in the Swin Transformer, we compute self-attention within local windows, but op-
                         erate in the frequency domain rather than directly in the spatial domain. Specifically,
                         given an input feature map, we first partition it into a set of non-overlapping spatial
                         windows. For each window, we apply a Fourier transform to obtain the corresponding
                         frequency representation, as illustrated in Figure 4. We then compute self-attention weights
                         within each frequency-domain window, allowing the module to down-weight redundant
                         low-frequency components related to fog and amplify mid-frequency and high-frequency
                         responses that encode object structures.

                         composed of global pooling and a sigmoid activation.
                               Intuitively, under dense fog conditions, contrast degradation suppresses
                         low-frequency dominant structures, causing the gating network to assign higher weights
                         to frequency-enhanced features. In contrast, under light fog or clear conditions, the
                         gating coefficient shifts towards the state-space branch, which provides stable global
                         contextual modeling.
                               Although explicit supervision is not applied to the gating coefficient, we observe that
                         the learned gate adaptively balances global context modeling and frequency compensation
                         across different fog densities, contributing to stable performance under severe degradation.

                         3.4. Object Detection Module Based on High-Frequency Awareness and Spatial Dependency
                              Although Mamba-based state space models provide efficient long-range dependency
                         modeling with linear computational complexity, they still exhibit clear weaknesses in foggy
                         small-object detection. Fog degradation is typically associated with the amplification of
                         low-frequency components and the suppression of high-frequency details. Due to recursive
                         state propagation, Mamba tends to emphasize dominant and smooth patterns, which
                         reduces its responsiveness to high-frequency cues such as edges and textures. Consequently,
                         restoring fine-grained features weakened by fog becomes difficult. Additionally, small
                         objects cover only a few pixels, and their representations can be easily over-smoothed
                         in deep networks, further impairing localization accuracy and classification reliability.
                         Moreover, conventional Feature Pyramid Networks (FPNs) support multi-scale fusion but
                         do not explicitly model cross-scale spatial dependencies, limiting contextual reasoning
                         when small targets are blurred and embedded in complex foggy backgrounds.
                              To overcome these limitations, we propose a Multi-Scale Frequency-Spatial Fusion
                         module, termed MFSF, which enhances detail cues and spatial context in fog-degraded
                         images through a coordinated design that includes a High-Frequency Perception module,
                         denoted as HFP, and a Spatial Dependency Perception module, denoted as SDP. The HFP
                         module explicitly extracts and strengthens high-frequency components such as edges and
                         textures through high-pass filtering, thereby suppressing low-frequency interference caused



                                                                                         https://doi.org/10.3390/sym18020320
Symmetry 2026, 18, 320                                                                                           12 of 28



                         by fog. The SDP module captures contextual correlations across feature levels by modeling
                         cross-scale spatial dependencies, improving feature alignment and semantic consistency.
                         With these two components, MFSF produces strengthened multi-scale representations and
                         enhances the robustness of small-object detection in challenging foggy scenes.

                         3.4.1. High-Frequency Perception Module
                               The proposed High-Frequency Perception (HFP) module is illustrated in Figure 5.
                         Under foggy conditions, high-frequency components of the image, such as edges and
                         textures, are often severely attenuated. Although the Mamba mechanism excels at modeling
                         sequential dependencies, its sensitivity to high-frequency details is relatively limited.
                         The HFP module is designed to compensate for this deficiency, forming a functionally
                         complementary relationship with the HSM-SSD module to jointly enhance the model’s
                         perception of small objects in foggy scenes. The HFP first applies a wavelet transform to the
                         input feature Ci to obtain the high-frequency response Fi , which has the same dimensions
                         as Ci . Subsequently, Fi is used as input to generate two sets of attention weights: channel
                         attention weights ucp and spatial attention weights usp . This process can be expressed as:

                         features into spatially localized frequency components, enabling simultaneous preservation
                         of edge, texture, and directional information. Unlike fixed high-pass filters that uniformly
                         amplify noise-sensitive frequencies, wavelet-based decomposition provides adaptive
                         multi-scale responses that are more robust to fog-induced degradation. (In all experiments,
                         the discrete wavelet transform is implemented using the Haar wavelet, which provides
                         a favorable balance between computational efficiency and edge-preserving capability for
                         real-time detection tasks.)

                         3.5. Loss Function
                              In object detection tasks, the loss function typically consists of multiple components,
                         including classification loss, bounding box regression loss, and optional auxiliary con-
                         straints. To ensure efficient and stable detection of small objects in foggy environments,
                         we design two types of loss functions: the standard loss function used in general object
                         detection, and a fog-aware loss function specifically tailored for fog-degraded images.

                         3.5.1. General Object Detection Loss Function
                               In general object detection tasks, the loss function consists of two components: classi-
                         fication loss and bounding box regression loss. Given the class label y and the bounding
                         box coordinates b = ( x, y, w, h) for each object, our goal is to minimize the errors in both
                         classification and regression. The specific loss function is defined as:

                                                             Ltotal = Lcls + Lloc                                   (35)

                         Here, the classification loss Lcls is typically implemented using cross-entropy loss, which
                         measures the discrepancy between the predicted class probabilities and the ground-truth
                         labels for each object:
                                                              Lcls = − ∑ yi log (ŷi )                           (36)
                                                                       i

                         Here, yi denotes the ground-truth label for the i-th class, and ŷi represents the predicted
                         probability from the model. The bounding box regression loss Lloc is typically implemented

Symmetry 2026, 18, 320                                                                                               15 of 28



                         using the smooth L1 loss, which measures the discrepancy between the predicted bounding
                         boxes and the ground-truth boxes:
                                                                                  

                                                                  i

                         Here, b̂i and bi denote the coordinates of the predicted and ground-truth bounding boxes,

                         3.5.2. Fog-Aware Loss Function
                               Under foggy conditions, image degradation is mainly manifested as the diffusion
                         of low-frequency components and the pronounced attenuation of high-frequency de-
                         tails such as edges and textures. This degradation directly weakens the expressive ca-
                         pacity of intermediate features in detection networks, making it harder to discriminate
                         small objects. Although the proposed frequency-windowed attention and multi-scale
                         frequency-spatial fusion modules can structurally alleviate fog interference, it is still nec-
                         essary during training to explicitly constrain the network and encourage richer detail
                         responses in regions severely affected by fog.

                                                         Lfog = λ · Ldet + α · Lcontrast ,

                         where Lcontrast adaptively increases the penalty for low-contrast regions based on estimated
                         fog density, encouraging stable optimization under severe visibility degradation.
                              Unlike focal loss or IoU-based losses that primarily address class imbalance or local-
                         ization accuracy, the proposed fog-aware loss explicitly regularizes low-contrast regions,
                         acting as a training-stage stabilization mechanism rather than a replacement for standard
                         detection objectives.
                              To this end, we propose a fog-aware loss function based on frequency-domain detail
                         regularization. Unlike conventional loss designs that rely on additional supervision signals
                         or pseudo-labels, this loss introduces no extra manual annotations. Instead, it acts as a
                         feature-level regularization term directly applied to intermediate feature representations
                         in the detection network. Specifically, the fog-aware loss is imposed on the feature maps
                         yi output by the Multi-Scale Frequency-Spatial Fusion (MFSF) module, with the goal of
                         strengthening high-frequency detail representations in fog-degraded regions.
                              First, to characterize high-frequency responses in the feature maps, we employ a
                         Laplacian operator to extract high-frequency components. The high-frequency response of
                         the feature map yi is defined as:
                                                                H (yi ) = |∆(yi )|                                (38)

                         Here, ∆(·) denotes the Laplacian operator, which is used to emphasize edges and texture
                         information in the feature maps.
                              Second, to adaptively emphasize regions severely affected by fog degradation, a
                         fog-weight map W is constructed. This weight map is automatically computed from local
                         contrast and gradient statistics of the input image. Regions with lower contrast and edge
                         strength are assigned larger weight values, thereby receiving higher importance in the loss
                         computation. The specific form of the fog-weight map can be expressed as:

                                               W ( p) = 1 + λ · σ(α(τc − C ( p)) + β(τe − E( p)))                       (39)

                         Here, C ( p) and E( p) denote the local contrast and gradient magnitude at pixel location p,
                         respectively; σ (·) is the Sigmoid function; τc and τe are adaptive thresholds; and λ, α, and
                         β are hyperparameters. Based on this, the fog-aware loss is defined as:

                                                             N∑
                                                  Lfog = −       (W ( p) − 1) · ∥ H (yi )( p)∥1                       (40)
                                                               p


                         Here, N denotes the number of pixels in the feature map. This loss encourages stronger
                         high-frequency responses in regions with higher fog weights, thereby suppressing the
                         over-smoothing of features caused by fog degradation.
                              Finally, the fog-aware loss is incorporated as a regularization term and jointly opti-
                         mized with the standard object detection loss. The overall loss function can be expressed as:

                                                         Ltotal = µ · Lfog + Lcls + Lloc                              (41)

                         Here, µ is the weighting coefficient for the fog-aware loss, used to balance the contributions

                         RTTS training set and subsequently validated on the Cityscapes-foggy benchmarks. Exper-
                         imental results indicate that perturbing these hyperparameters within a ±20% range leads
                         to performance variations of less than 0.3 mAP, suggesting that the proposed fog-aware
                         loss exhibits stable behavior across datasets and fog intensities.

                                5. Conclusions
                                     This study presented HS-MambaDet, a frequency-compensated state-space detec-
                                tor for small-object detection in foggy and dynamic environments. The proposed
                                method embedded a lightweight Frequency-Window-Attention state-space module, termed
                                FWS-SSD, into the backbone to enable efficient long-range modeling. In FWS-SSD, channel
                                mixing and gating were performed in a low-dimensional hidden-state space to reduce
                                computational cost, while frequency-window attention enhances high-frequency details
                                and suppresses low-frequency fog interference, mitigating detail loss caused by recursive
                                state propagation. Additionally, a Multi-Scale Frequency-Spatial Fusion (MFSF) module
                                was introduced in the neck to strengthen fine-grained cues and cross-scale spatial con-
                                text. A fog-aware detection loss was further designed to emphasize low-contrast and
                                detail-deficient regions, improving detection stability under fog degradation. Experiments
                                on RTTS and CityScapes verified the effectiveness of the proposed approach. HS-MambaDet
                                achieves 85.2% mAP@0.5 and 55.1% mAP@0.5–0.95 on RTTS, outperforms representative
                                one-stage, two-stage, and state-space baselines, and maintains competitive inference effi-
                                ciency, demonstrating a favorable accuracy-efficiency trade-off for real-time foggy-scene
                                small-object detection.
