## Abstract

The existing image compression methods are designed for the human visual system. They can achieve good compression quality for low-frequency components of the image that are important to human vision. However, for object detection models, both high and low-frequency components are essential. As a result, the detection metrics on the compressed images obtained by current methods will decline. Particularly for small object detection, the lack of high-frequency signals makes it difficult to distinguish the targets from the background. In this paper, we propose a frequency-separated image compression model, named FSIC. During the training process, the compression of low-frequency components only employs MSE loss, while the compression of high-frequency components additionally incorporates a detection loss. We validate FSIC's image compression capability for the small object detection task on the VisDrone dataset and Dota dataset. Results show that under extremely high compression rates, FSIC demonstrates a better performance compared with current compression methods. Furthermore, FSIC has the fastest encoding speed among current learning-based compression models.

## Introduction

Vision-based object detection is an important requirement for many cyber-physical systems like UAVs and AGVs. However, it remains challenging due to the limited computing capabilities of the edge devices. An effective way to address the issue is to transmit the image to the cloud with more powerful computing resources for object detection. Unfortunately, in normal cases, the link bandwidth for such a transmission is limited. A promising approach is to compress the image in situ and then transmit it. Standard image codecs [1], [2], [3] achieve data compression by reducing the redundancy between pixels and removing some information that is not sensitive to human eyes [4]; thus, they are not suitable for object detection, as some high-frequency signals are lost.

The learning-based compression models with autoregressive context [5], [6] have surpassed the leading standard image codec—BPG (Better Portable Graphics). These methods are based on transform coding and utilize convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to learn nonlinear functions that can map images into more compressible latent representations. The whole coding framework resembles an autoencoder, consisting of an encoder that reduces the dimensionality of the images and a decoder that acts as an approximate inverse function to map the latent representations back to images. Besides the encoder and the decoder, there is an entropy model that estimates the probability distribution of latent representations. With the probability, entropy coding can be used to further compress the latents. However, while the autoregressive entropy model makes the estimated probabilities more aligned with reality, it significantly slows down the speed of encoding and decoding. Parallelizable entropy models [7], [8] have been proposed to address this issue, enhancing operational speed while ensuring compression quality. Recently, the attention mechanism [9] has also been used to enhance the effectiveness of compression models.

The aforementioned standard image codecs and learning-based compression methods are designed for human vision. At a high compression ratio, a large amount of high-frequency information that is not sensitive to human eyes might be discarded. However, the CNNs in the object detection model will use the high-frequency components (HFC) in images [10]. The loss of HFC will lead to a decline in performance for object detection tasks. Especially for small targets, when HFC is lost, they become difficult to distinguish from the background.

To solve the above problem, compression methods need to be improved to meet the needs of object detection tasks. Compared with traditional image codecs, learning-based compression methods are easier to optimize for specific tasks. The most intuitive approach is to jointly train the compression network with the corresponding task network [11]. Given the significance of HFC in small object detection, we try to improve the compression process of HFC. Fritsche et al. [12] proposed to separate the low-frequency components (LFC) and high-frequency components (HFC) of the input image when performing image super-resolution reconstruction with neural networks and train them in different ways. In this work, we first investigate the compression performance of existing compression networks on LFC and HFC using object detection task results as the evaluation metric. Then we propose the frequency-separated image compression model (FSIC) which extends the joint training framework by incorporating a frequency separation learning strategy to train the compression network.

We conduct frequency separation on the VisDrone dataset [13] and utilize the existing learning-based compression method [6] to compress the original image, LFC, and HFC respectively. Then we use YOLOv5 [14] to perform detection on them and get the following findings:

  * •

Both the LFC and HFC of an image are utilized by the object detection model.

  * •

Existing learning-based compression methods using MSE as the loss function demonstrate favorable compression performances on LFC, but their compression performances on HFC are not satisfactory.

Based on the above findings, we propose FSIC. Specifically, we employ a linear filter to separate the LFC and HFC of the input image in the training process. Then, the MSE loss is used to guide the compression of both LFC and HFC. The compression of HFC is further optimized through joint training with a detection model. FSIC exhibits excellent compression performance for object detection tasks on the small object dataset. For extremely high compression ratios, our model has advantages compared with existing compression methods. Moreover, the encoding speed of FSIC is faster than that of the compared learning-based compression models.

### 2.3. Lightweight Models for Edge Deployment

Beyond distillation, complementary compression techniques include structured and unstructured pruning [7], post-training and quantization-aware training [8], and lightweight architecture design [9]. MobileNetV2 [48] and MobileViT [49] exemplify architecture-level efficiency through depth-wise separable convolutions and hybrid transformer-convolution blocks, respectively, achieving competitive accuracy at a fraction of the parameter count of standard ResNets [50]. Knowledge distillation is particularly attractive in conjunction with lightweight architectures because it can recover accuracy lost during architectural simplification without modifying the inference graph. Several recent works demonstrate that distilled lightweight detectors can achieve real-time performance on Jetson-class embedded processors and FPGA platforms while meeting the mAP requirements of practical wildfire monitoring systems [51,52]. The present study targets the Faster R-CNN [6] family with a ResNet-18 [50] student backbone, balancing detection accuracy with deployment feasibility on edge hardware.

In summary, three observations motivate the present work. First, although deep detectors have decisively outperformed handcrafted-feature pipelines for fire and smoke recognition [24,25], their parameter and FLOP budgets remain incompatible with real-time inference on UAV edge hardware [51,52]. Second, knowledge distillation is an attractive route to compression because it leaves the student’s inference graph unchanged [10,11], yet the prevailing detection-distillation methods—including the most recent remote-sensing frameworks ARSD [45], AFD [46], Two-Way [47], and DPGD [20]—apply their supervision over dense feature maps and were not designed for the specific statistics of aerial smoke. Third, aerial smoke simultaneously exhibits extreme foreground sparsity, diffuse low-contrast boundaries, and strong appearance similarity to non-fire backgrounds, a combination that exposes three weaknesses of uniform feature regression: imbalance-dominated gradients, boundary-noise over-penalization, and sensitivity to teacher–student feature-magnitude divergence. The research gap addressed here is therefore the absence of a distillation framework that (i) explicitly separates foreground and background supervision to neutralize the imbalance, and (ii) transfers a magnitude-invariant, relational representation rather than absolute feature values. DeTD is designed to fill precisely this gap.

Several papers deal with the impact of lossy compression on object localization and classification [29,30,31,39,40,41]. The paper [41] is one of the first to address object detection in JPEG-compressed images. It shows that full decompression might not be needed, and an object can be detected using DCT coefficients’ processing by the pre-trained SSD network. The authors of the paper [29] state that there are many datasets in which the images contained in these datasets are already lossy compressed, and this can result in certain problems in CNN training. They also state that considerable reduction in performance can be observed for large CR. The MobileNetV2, VGG16, VGG19, ResNet50, NASNetMobile and NASNetLarge classifiers are studied in [39], and it is demonstrated that lossy compression using atomic wavelets has practically no negative impact on classification for visually lossless image compression. The authors of [30] apply CNN to the most important DCT coefficients and show that the designed network classifier is quite resilient to JPEG quality, but its performance depends on spatial resolution. The authors of [40] consider five types of distortions, namely blur, noise, contrast, JPEG, and JPEG2000 compression, and show that modern networks are sensitive to these distortion types, particularly to blur and noise. Note that distortions due to lossy compression are, in some sense, similar to noise and blur [42], although statistical and spatial spectral characteristics of distortions due to lossy compression considerably depend on the coder used, CR, and image properties.

##### Summary

This paper addresses the challenge of designing efficient framework for real-time object detection and image compression. The proposed Compressive Convolutional Network (CCN) is basically a compressive-sensing-enabled convolutional neural network. Instead of designing different components for compressive sensing and object detection, the CCN optimizes and reuses the convolution operation for recoverable data embedding and image compression. Technically, the incoherence condition, which is the sufficient condition for recoverable data embedding, is incorporated in the first convolutional layer of the CCN model as regularization; Therefore, the CCN convolution kernels learned by training over the VOC and COCO image set can be used for data embedding and image compression.
