L12@P0: ABSTRACT
L13@P0: Object detection in unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) aerial images has become increasingly important in military
L14@P0: and civil applications. General object detection models are not robust enough against interclass similarity and
L15@P0: intraclass variability of small objects, and UAV-specific nuisances such as uncontrolled weather conditions. Unlike
L16@P0: previous approaches focusing on high-level semantic information, we report the importance of underlying features
L17@P0: to improve detection accuracy and robustness from the information-theoretic perspective. Specifically, we propose
L18@P0: a robust and discriminative feature learning approach through mutual information maximization (RD-MIM),
L19@P0: which can be integrated into numerous object detection methods for aerial images. Firstly, we present the rank
L20@P0: sample mining method to reduce underlying feature differences between the natural image domain and the aerial
L21@P0: image domain. Then, we design a momentum contrast learning strategy to make object features similar to the
L22@P0: same category and dissimilar to different categories. Finally, we construct a transformer-based global attention
L23@P0: mechanism to boost object location semantics by leveraging the high interrelation of different receptive fields. We
L24@P0: conduct extensive experiments on the VisDrone and Unmanned Aerial Vehicle Benchmark Object Detection and
L25@P0: Tracking (UAVDT) datasets to prove the effectiveness of the proposed method. The experimental results show that
L26@P0: our approach brings considerable robustness gains to basic detectors and advanced detection methods, achieving
L27@P0: relative growth rates of 51.0% and 39.4% in corruption robustness, respectively. Our code is available at https://
L28@P0: github.com/cq100/RD-MIM (accessed on 2 August 2024).

L31@P0: 1 Introduction
L32@P0: Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) collecting high-resolution images have a wide range of applications in many fields, such as traffic management, disaster response, and infrastructure inspection [1,2].
L33@P0: Using object detection approaches to understand and analyze aerial images has attracted significant
L34@P0: attention [3]. In recent years, deep neural networks (DNNs) have developed rapidly in object detection,
L35@P0-1: which achieved superior performance on natural images [4].4150 CMC, 2024, vol.80, no.3
L36@P1: Directly applying these detection models to aerial images still has two problems. On the one hand,
L37@P1: interclass similarity and intraclass variability are more severe on aerial image datasets, impairing the
L38@P1: accuracy of object detection [5,6]. Fig. 1a intuitively illustrates that some objects indeed have confusing
L39@P1: appearances and low distinctiveness. On the other hand, UAVs have complex operating environments,
L40@P1: such as varying light, motion blur, and uncontrolled weather conditions, which make object detection
L41@P1: even harder [7]. As depicted in Fig. 1b, changing illumination drastically affects object visibility. To
L42@P1: deal with these problems, a great amount of effort has been carried out [8,9]. Nevertheless, these efforts
L43@P1: often ignore the importance of underlying features. For example, the dual sampler and head detection
L44@P1: network (DSHNet) [8] designed class-biased samplers and bilateral box heads from the perspective of
L45@P1: high-level semantic information to process semantically similar objects. Although it allowed detectors
L46@P1: to perform better for each category on clean aerial images, the robustness was poor in highly variable
L47@P1: capturing conditions.

L49@P1: similarity and intraclass variability. (b) UAV complex operating environments (daytime vs. nighttime)
L50@P1: Transfer learning available everywhere is an effective scheme to learn underlying features. It
L51@P1: not only accelerates model convergence but also tremendously improves model robustness [10].
L52@P1: Common transfer learning methods use the mature backbone trained on the ImageNet dataset [11]
L53@P1: as initialization and freeze part of the layers to retain more robust underlying features [12]. When we
L54@P1: consider transfer learning from the ImageNet backbone to object detection models in aerial images,
L55@P1: the obtained results are not as desirable as described in the leading papers [10,13]. As shown in Fig. 2,
L56@P1: in the case of excluding model overfitting, the accuracy of the loading ImageNet method is similar
L57@P1: to training from scratch, and robustness performance is even worse with the increase of iterations.
L58@P1: We assume the reason for this phenomenon is that the underlying features are poorly learned due to
L59@P1: the significant differences between natural images and aerial images. The differences may stem from
L60@P1: the different heights and angles captured by UAV cameras, leading to smaller and denser objects in
L61@P1: aerial images.
L62@P1: To this end, we propose a robust and discriminative underlying feature learning approach
L63@P1: according to the mutual information maximization principle, which is able to assist object detectors for
L64@P1: aerial images to further improve robustness rather than achieve state-of-the-art performance. Similar
L65@P1: to the robustness measure method in natural images [13], we also add various corruption types to
L66@P1: aerial image datasets according to the UAV operation environments. The experiments are conducted
L67@P1: on clean and corrupted VisDrone [14] and Unmanned Aerial Vehicle Benchmark Object Detection
L68@P1: and Tracking (UAVDT) [15] datasets. Fig. 2 intuitively shows the great performance gains of our
L69@P1: approach, especially on corrupted aerial images. Our code is available at https://github.com/cq100/

L89@P3: 2 Related Work
L90@P3: 2.1 General Object Detection
L91@P3: With the introduction of region-based convolutional neural network (R-CNN) model, DNNbased networks have played a central role in general object detection tasks [16]. There are primarily
L92@P3: two streams of object detection approaches: anchor-free and anchor-based detectors [17]. Anchorfree detectors use the key points to estimate the predicted bounding box, such as fully convolutional
L93@P3: one-stage object detection (FCOS) [18]. Anchor-based detectors locate object positions by generating
L94@P3: anchor candidates, which can be further divided into two-stage and one-stage detectors [19]. For
L95@P3: example, compared with the representative one-stage method RetinaNet [20], Faster R-CNN [21]
L96@P3: involves a region proposal network (RPN) to search for possible object locations. We measured the
L97@P3: proposed method on three different types of detectors.
L98@P3: From the component perspective, DNN-based object detectors generally consist of three parts:
L99@P3: the backbone, the neck, and the head [22]. The backbone plays a key role, which has strong feature
L100@P3: extraction capability, such as the traditional ResNet50 [23], the popular RegNet [24], and the latest
L101@P3: PVTv2 (pyramid vision transformer version 2) [25]. RegNet can balance accuracy and computation
L102@P3: amount well, which is conducive to designing lightweight networks. PVTv2 improves the current
L103@P3: promising pyramid vision transformer to establish a more robust and flexible baseline. We use the
L104@P3: three networks with different structures as the backbone of detectors.
L105@P3: 2.2 Object Detection in Aerial Images
L106@P3: Compared with natural images, object detection for aerial images brings greater challenges.
L107@P3: Interclass similarity and intraclass variability degrade the performance of general detectors.
L108@P3: Huang et al. [26] constructed a multi-proxy detection network by analyzing object distribution for
L109@P3: aerial images to deal with serious category confusion. Wang et al. [27] used a class-aware strategy to
L110@P3: maintain a balance in the number of objects from different categories, which facilitated the distinction
L111@P3-4: of confused objects. Tang et al. [5] designed the points estimated network based on a coarse-to-fineCMC, 2024, vol.80, no.3 4153
L112@P4: framework to predict the positions of similar objects. These customized approaches are limited due to
L113@P4: non-universality, which cannot be effectively applied to common object detection methods.
L114@P4: Object detection methods for aerial images require detectors with strong robustness due to
L115@P4: UAV complex working conditions [28]. Existing potential solutions generally can be divided into
L116@P4: three aspects [29,30]. First, adversarial training frameworks are utilized to obtain robustness gains.
L117@P4: Wu et al. [31] used UAV meta-data in conjunction with the detector based on adversarial learning
L118@P4: to extract domain-robust features. Second, the expert head is designed on the top of the model for
L119@P4: each specific domain, such as capturing time and weather [29]. As more domains are involved, model
L120@P4: ensemble becomes difficult, and it cannot be generalized to unseen domains. Last, data augmentation
L121@P4: is a typical strategy to enhance the robustness of detectors. Dense and small object detection (DSDet)
L122@P4: [9] used data augmentation based on crop functionality to increase training data, achieving robust
L123@P4: detection in aerial images.
L124@P4: Besides the aforementioned methods, there exist other advanced techniques for aerial object
L125@P4: detection. Blas et al. [32] designed a swimming pool detection platform by utilizing geographic
L126@P4: information tools and common machine learning methods. Xie et al. [33] presented a plug-and-play
L127@P4: object activation network (OAN) to help detectors focus on patches containing objects in aerial images.
L128@P4: Task-wise sampling convolutions (TS-Conv) [34] guided dynamic label assignment by adaptively
L129@P4: sampling features from sensitive areas for localization and classification, boosting detector adaptation
L130@P4: and resistance.
L131@P4: 2.3 Transfer Learning in Object Detection for Aerial Images
L132@P4: Using transfer learning to improve the performance of DNN-based models is a common practice,
L133@P4: because underlying features are close to each other when the input domains of the tasks are similar
L134@P4: [35,36]. Specifically, models transfer the structure or parameters from the pre-trained network trained
L135@P4: on the ImageNet dataset available publicly [37]. Generally speaking, there exist three transfer learning
L136@P4: methods. The first is full-network transfer only using the pre-trained network as initialization, which
L137@P4: easily erases the robustness property during fine-tuning. The second is fixed-feature transfer, which
L138@P4: freezes the whole backbone and only trains the detection head. It performs slightly worse, but
L139@P4: facilitates robustness transfer [13]. The third is a common approach that fixes part of the pre-trained
L140@P4: backbone and loads parameters as initialization, which is usually applied for object detection models
L141@P4: in aerial images [22,38].
L142@P4: We find that directly transferring ImageNet backbone to detectors for aerial images yields
L143@P4: poor performance gains. The possible reason is that the complex capturing conditions of UAVs
L144@P4: make the differences between natural images and aerial images larger, which leads to fewer similar
L145@P4: underlying features.
