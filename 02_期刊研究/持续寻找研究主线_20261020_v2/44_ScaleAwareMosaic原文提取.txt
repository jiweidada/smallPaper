                                         Abstract
                                         The performance of deep learning-based object detection methods is heavily dependent
                                         on the characteristics of training data. However, real-world detection datasets often
                                         exhibit severe imbalance in object-scale distributions, resulting in insuﬃcient supervision
                                         for object sizes with limited instances. To address this issue, we propose a scale-aware
                                         mosaic augmentation algorithm and a generalized scale-adaptive intersection over union
                                         (GSIoU)-based varifocal loss (VFL) function. The proposed scale-aware mosaic
                                         augmentation method alleviates object-scale imbalance by explicitly regulating scale
                                         distributions during training sample construction, overcoming the tendency of
                                         conventional mosaic augmentation to preserve inherent dataset imbalance. Furthermore,
                                         to handle the limitation of existing IoU-based quality targets that impose relatively large
                                         penalties for small objects, we replace the localization quality target in VFL with GSIoU,
                                         thereby enabling more consistent classification performance across object scales. We
                                         evaluate the eﬀectiveness of the proposed method by applying the proposed method to
                                         RT-DETRv2 and conducting experiments on the HRSC2016-MS dataset. Experimental
                                         results demonstrate that the proposed method improves the overall average precision
                                         (AP) by 1.11AP over the baseline, with a particularly notable improvement of 16.43AP in
                                         small-object average precision, confirming that the proposed approach achieves balanced
                                         detection performance across object scales.

                                         Keywords: object detection; scale imbalance; data augmentation; mosaic augmentation;
                                         varifocal loss; small object detection

                             insuﬃciently sampled objects receive weaker supervision during training, and this
                             limitation disproportionately aﬀects small objects.

                             challenging because it occupies only a limited number of pixels relative to the overall
                             image resolution. Therefore, small objects are inherently sensitive to annotation noise and
                             matching errors during training. Compared to large objects, even minor localization
                             inaccuracies can result in substantial relative misalignment for small objects, leading to
                             unstable positive sample assignment and weakened supervision signals. Motivated by
                             this concern, several IoU variants have been proposed to provide more informative

                             2. Related Works
                             2.1. Challenges of Object Scale in Detection
                                  In the COCO detection benchmark [6], object sizes are defined based on the bounding
                             box area measured in pixels, where small objects have an area smaller than 32 × 32 ,
                             medium objects fall between 32 × 32 and 96 × 96 , and large objects exceed 96 × 96 .
                             Object detection performance is strongly influenced by object scale and its distribution, as
                             objects of diﬀerent sizes contribute unevenly to training and optimization. Objects from
                             sparsely populated size ranges contribute less to the training process, leading to limited
                             performance gains.
                                  In addition to this, small objects are inherently more sensitive to spatial perturbations
                             introduced by annotation noise and localization inaccuracies [13]. This leads to substantial
                             degradation in overlap-based matching, regression targets, and evaluation metrics that
                             depend on spatial alignment. As a result, small objects are less likely to produce reliable
                             positive samples during training, further weakening their supervision signals [14]. In
                             contrast, objects with large areas tend to exert a disproportionate influence on the training
                             process, biasing optimization away from balanced learning across all objects. Large
                             objects provide stronger and more stable positive supervision signals and dominate
                             gradient updates, thereby biasing the optimization process toward larger object scales
                             [15,16]. To mitigate these diﬃculties, existing research eﬀorts can be broadly categorized
                             into two directions: (i) modifying the input image resolution or scale, and (ii) improving
                             model architectures to enhance feature representations.
                                  From the perspective of input scale manipulation, prior studies have investigated a
                             variety of data augmentation strategies that adjust the eﬀective object size seen by the
                             detector during training. Cropping-based augmentation strategies enlarge target objects
                             within the input image, enabling them to occupy a greater spatial extent and thereby
                             receive stronger and more focused supervision during training [17–20]. Along this line,
                             YOLOv4 [7] introduces mosaic augmentation, which combines four diﬀerent images into
                             a single composite image. By randomly scaling and arranging multiple images within a

                                                                                      https://doi.org/10.3390/electronics15051075
Electronics 2026, 15, 1075                                                                                               4 of 18




                             single training sample, mosaic augmentation allows objects of diﬀerent sizes to be
                             observed simultaneously. This strategy generally improves small-object detection
                             performance by resizing objects to smaller scales through packing multiple images into a
                             single input. YOLO9000 [8] introduced a multi-scale training strategy by randomly
                             varying the input image resolution during training to improve robustness to scale
                             variations, and it has been widely adopted and extended in subsequent YOLO-based
                             detectors. Although these strategies increase scale diversity through data augmentation,
                             they largely preserve the inherent imbalance in object-scale distribution and therefore do
                             not explicitly address scale distribution imbalance at the dataset level.
                                  In contrast, other works have been carried out to improve multi-scale object detection
                             performance by modifying the model architecture itself, explicitly enhancing feature
                             representations across diﬀerent spatial resolutions. This enables objects of varying sizes
                             to be handled within a unified framework [21,22]. Pyramid-based architectures improved
                             cross-scale feature fusion to strengthen information flow between low-level spatial details
                             and high-level semantic representations by adopting top-down pathways, lateral
                             connections, and bidirectional fusion mechanisms [8,9,23,24]. Beyond feature fusion,
                             several studies explicitly model scale diversity within the backbone or neck by modifying
                             receptive fields or branch structures. TridentNet [25] introduces a multi-branch backbone
                             architecture in which parallel branches share the same weights but employ diﬀerent
                             dilation rates, thereby generating scale-specific feature maps with matched receptive
                             fields for objects of diﬀerent sizes. In [26], scale-aware modeling strategies are explored to
                             improve robustness under fine-grained variations in signal-based regression tasks.
                             SCAFNet [27] proposed a CNN–transformer hybrid network to enhance robustness under
                             scale-related feature inconsistencies by compensating semantic discrepancies between
                             local and global representations through adaptive feature fusion. Recently, CNN layers
                             have been incorporated into transformer architectures to improve multiscale feature
                             representation and enhance robustness under scale variations [28]. Although these
                             architectural refinements primarily enhance feature representation across scales, the
                             underlying imbalance in scale-wise supervision signals remains unaddressed. This
                             limitation leads to fewer eﬀective supervision signals for objects from underrepresented
                             object scales during training, thereby biasing the optimization process toward dominant
                             scales.

                             2.2. Localization-Aware Classification
                                  RT-DETR [29] is a real-time end-to-end object detector built upon the DETR [30]
                             architecture, which has been widely adopted and extended in subsequent real-time
                             detection research. One of the key contributions of RT-DETR is the introduction of
                             uncertainty-minimal query selection mechanism, which explicitly considers both
                             classification confidence and localization quality when selecting encoder features as object
                             queries. When DETR-based detectors employ encoder feature selection, existing query
                             selection methods primarily rely on classification confidence, while localization quality is
                             not explicitly considered. Unlike conventional query selection strategies, RT-DETR
                             integrates localization information into the classification objective by adopting VFL [31].
                             Therefore, the classification scores are influenced not only by classification confidence but
                             also by the degree of spatial alignment between predicted and ground-truth boxes. After
                             the success application in RT-DETR, this kind of classification loss design has been widely
                             adopted in subsequent object detection studies [32–34].
                                  VFL is an extension of the focal loss function [35], introduced to mitigate the adverse
                             eﬀect of an overwhelming number of easy negative samples during training. It assigns the
                             IoU between the predicted and the ground-truth bounding boxes as a quality target for
                             positive samples, aligning classification confidence with localization quality.

                             [0,1] indicates the IoU between the predicted bounding box and the ground-truth box.
                             The parameter 𝛾 controls the balance between easy and hard samples, while 𝛼 adjusts
                             the weighting between foreground and background samples.
                                   Although IoU serves as a key component in VFL, its use introduces inherent
                             limitations. Conventional IoU assigns zero to non-overlapping boxes regardless of their
                             spatial distance, failing to penalize larger localization errors more severely. Moreover, IoU
                             is highly sensitive to object scale, causing small objects to receive disproportionately large
                             penalties for the same localization error. These issues hinder the achievement of scale-
                             robust detection performance.

                             2.3. Overlap-Based Localization Metrics
                                  IoU is the most widely used metric for measuring the spatial alignment between
                             predicted and ground-truth bounding boxes in object detection. It is defined as the ratio
                             between the area of intersection and the area of union of two boxes. Owing to its simplicity
                             and clear geometric interpretation, IoU-based losses have become a standard component
                             in modern object detectors. However, when predicted and ground-truth boxes do not
                             overlap, IoU yields zero values and fails to provide informative gradient signals,
                             regardless of their relative spatial distance or configuration.
                                  To address this issue, generalized intersection over union (GIoU) [36] was proposed
                             as an extension of IoU that incorporates the spatial enclosure between two boxes. GIoU
                             leverages the area of the smallest enclosing box to quantify how far the predicted and
                             ground-truth boxes are separated when they do not overlap. As a result, GIoU provides
                             meaningful gradients even when boxes do not overlap, enabling the loss function to
                             encode relative spatial distance and enclosure relationships. The mathematical definition
                             of GIoU is defined as [33]:

                                                               GIoU = IoU −                 ,

                             represents the smallest enclosing box that fully contains both 𝐴 and 𝐵 . This property
                             significantly improves optimization stability under zero-overlap conditions and has led
                             to its adoption in bounding box regression losses across various detection frameworks.

                             inherent scale sensitivity of IoU, which disproportionately penalizes localization errors
                             for small objects. More specifically, SIoU introduces a scale-adaptive formulation that

                             3. Proposed Method
                                 The proposed method consists of two main components: a scale-aware mosaic
                             augmentation strategy that balances the scale distribution of the dataset, and a GSIoU-
                             based VFL that improved localization quality of classification loss for small objects.

                             3.1. Scale-Aware Mosaic Augmentation
                                  In this paper, we propose a scale-aware mosaic augmentation strategy shown in
                             Figure 3. This strategy retains the standard mosaic construction pipeline but explicitly
                             controls the scale distribution. The proposed mosaic algorithm consists of the following
                             four processing steps:
                                  In Step 1, all training and test images are resized to match the model’s input
                             resolution, and the corresponding ground-truth bounding boxes are also rescaled
                             accordingly.
                                  In Step 2, each object is categorized as small, medium, or large based on the area of
                             its ground-truth bounding box according to the COCO detection benchmark [6]. The
                                                                                          https://doi.org/10.3390/electronics15051075
Electronics 2026, 15, 1075                                                                                                    7 of 18




                             number of objects in each scale group is counted, and the maximum object count among
                             the three scales is selected as the 𝑁       .
                                  In Step 3, an image that contains an object in the scale group whose object count has
                             not yet reached the 𝑁           is selected. Then, a patch with a size equal to half of the
                             model’s input resolution is cropped, with the selected object placed near the center of the
                             patch using positional jittering. After patch cropping, annotations that are excessively
                             truncated within the patch are discarded. That is, bounding boxes whose remaining
                             visible area or width or height in the cropped region falls below a predefined threshold
                             are removed to prevent noisy supervision and unstable training. Examples of bounding
                             boxes filtered out by this procedure are shown in Figure 4. Also, patches that contain
                             objects belonging to scale groups that have already reached 𝑁             are identified as
                             invalid patches and excluded from the candidate patch set. This invalid patch selection
                             mechanism suppresses the selection probability of patches containing objects with
                             suﬃcient counts and prevents overly aggressive augmentation during training.

                             mosaic images balance the object size distribution across the entire dataset.

                                  In Step 4, patches that were constructed in Step 3 are assembled into 2 × 2 mosaic
                             images with a final resolution of 640 × 640 , with annotations adjusted accordingly.
                             Mosaic construction is repeated until the object counts of all scale groups reach the 𝑁     .
                                  In contrast to conventional mosaic augmentation, which passively inherits scale
                             distributions from source images, our approach explicitly controls the scale distribution
                             of training samples by regulating object selection during patch selection. By augmenting
                             object samples belonging to underrepresented scale ranges, this strategy increases their
                             eﬀective sampling frequency and thereby provides more balanced supervision during
                             training. This strategy can be seamlessly integrated with any existing object detection
                             frameworks. Moreover, since it is performed prior to training, it introduces no additional
                             computation or memory overhead during the training process. The detailed procedure of
                             the proposed scale-aware mosaic augmentation is summarized in Algorithm A1 in
                             Appendix A.

                             proposed method. While the conventional approach preserves the original object-scale

                             prioritizing underrepresented object scales, resulting in a more balanced scale distribution
                             in the augmented samples.

                             3.2. GSIoU-Based VFL
                                  In this work, we improve VFL by replacing the IoU-based quality target with GSIoU
                             [35] to achieve detection performance that is robust and invariant to object scale. By
                             adopting GSIoU as the quality target, the classification branch can receive continuous and
                             scale-adaptive supervision signals even when predicted and ground-truth boxes do not
                             overlap. As GSIoU jointly encodes spatial enclosure and object-scale information, it allows
                             the quality target to reflect both relative spatial proximity and scale-dependent tolerance,
                             thereby alleviating excessive penalization of small objects. Consequently, classification
                             confidence is more consistently aligned with localization quality across different object
                             scales.

                             range [−1, 1] to [0, 1], ensuring compatibility with the original VFL formulation. Using this
                             mapping, the quality target 𝑞 used in VFL is reformulated as:

                             4. Experiments
                             4.1. Training Details
                                  In the experiments, we apply the proposed method to RT-DETRv2 detection
                             framework and train the model on the HRSC2016-MS dataset [5], which contains a single
                             object category. The dataset consists of satellite images collected from diverse maritime
                             scenes, including harbors and open sea areas, with targets ranging from large vessels to
                             small boats. Since the dataset contains a single object category with a highly imbalanced
                             scale distribution, it provides a controlled experimental setting in which the proposed
                             method can be evaluated directly, without the influence of inter-class detection diﬃculty.
                                  First, we apply the proposed scale-aware mosaic augmentation to the dataset to
                             construct augmented training samples. As illustrated in Figure 6, the proposed
                             augmentation rebalances the object-scale distribution by increasing the number of small-
                             and medium-scale objects.




                             Figure 6. Comparison of object count distributions across scales before and after applying the
                             proposed scale-aware mosaic augmentation.

                                  For the augmented dataset, the model is trained using the AdamW optimizer with a
                             cosine learning rate scheduler with a flat warmup phase. In addition to the proposed
                             augmentation method, we also apply the standard data augmentation techniques used in
                             RT-DETRv2, including the data augmentation warmup strategy, along with multi-scale
                             training with a base resolution of 640 × 640. In Step 3 of the proposed augmentation
                             method, the visible area threshold is set to 0.3, and the minimum width and height are set
                             to 10 pixels. The jittering range is defined as 0.2 of the patch size centered at the object. The
                             hyperparameters of GSIoU are set to 𝛾 = 0 and 𝜅 = 64. All remaining hyperparameters,
                             including optimizer settings and training configurations not explicitly mentioned above,
                             follow the default settings of the original RT-DETRv2 implementation. To obtain scale-wise

                             and re-evaluate it using the COCO benchmark protocol to compute metrics for small,

                             imbalance in supervision signals during training.

                             4.4. Comparisons with Real-Time Detectors
                                  We also compared the proposed method against recent state-of-the-art real-time
                             object detectors, including the M, L, and X variants of YOLOv8, YOLOv10 [34] and
                             YOLOv12 [38]. YOLOv8 adopts traditional 4-image mosaic augmentation together with
                             MixUp and copy-paste and disables mosaic augmentation in the later stage of training.
                             YOLOv10 and YOLOv12 also adopt this standard augmentation pipeline. In direct
                             comparison with YOLOv12-X, a representative YOLO-based SOTA real-time detector, the
                             proposed method achieves substantially higher accuracy, improving the overall AP by
                             3.91 AP, which corresponds to a 6.59% relative improvement, and improving small-object

                             AP by 6.75 AP, corresponding to a 34.6% relative improvement. Moreover, compared with
                             YOLOv8-L, which has a similar number of parameters, the proposed method achieves
                             10.10 AP higher overall accuracy and 9.94 AP higher performance on small objects.
                                  While YOLO-based models are designed to be general-purpose real-time object
                             detectors that optimize overall detection performance across diverse object sizes and
                             scenes, the proposed approach focuses on analyzing the object-scale distribution of the
                             used dataset and enabling small objects to be detected more eﬀectively. In terms of data
                             augmentation, YOLO uses random 4-image mosaic augmentation, through which the
                             original dataset distribution is directly reflected, causing large and medium objects to
                             continue occupying a dominant proportion during training. In contrast, the proposed
                             scale-aware mosaic augmentation encourages small objects to participate in training more.
                             There are also diﬀerences in loss function design. The YOLO models use a binary cross-
                             entropy loss in the classification branch, where localization errors and non-overlapping
                             predictions are treated as the same negative samples. In contrast, the proposed GSIoU-
                             based varifocal loss used in this study provides stable supervision signals even for small
                             objects. Although recent YOLO variants have steadily improved small-object detection
                             performance, the proposed method consistently delivers superior accuracy without
                             increasing model scale or the number of parameters.

                             5. Conclusions
                                   To enhance the robustness of object detectors under the scale distribution imbalance
                             in real-world datasets, we developed scale-aware mosaic augmentation and GSIoU-based
                             VFL. The scale-aware mosaic augmentation was designed to alleviate scale imbalance
                             during training by explicitly controlling object scales during mosaic construction, thereby
                             providing balanced scale-wise supervision signals. The GSIoU-based VFL improves the
                             quality of localization-aware supervision in the classification loss by jointly accounting for
                             object scale and non-overlapping spatial relationships, thereby providing more consistent
                             supervision across object sizes. These two improvements jointly enhance the training
                             eﬃciency and detection performance of RT-DETRv2, with particularly notable gains in
                             small-object detection. Experimental results demonstrated that the proposed method
                             achieves superior performance compared to the original RT-DETRv2 with fewer training
                             iterations, while introducing no additional parameters or computational overhead. These
                             attributes establish that the proposed method provides an eﬀective solution for detection
                             scenarios where the scale distribution of the training dataset is highly imbalanced.
                                   As future work, we will extend our research to the design of loss functions that
                             explicitly account for object-scale characteristics, enabling scale-aware supervision at the
                             loss level in addition to data augmentation.

                                   object counts have not yet reached the target. Patches are cropped around the selected
                                   objects with positional jittering to increase spatial diversity. To avoid introducing noisy
                                   supervision, object annotations that are excessively truncated or whose remaining visible
                                   size falls below predefined thresholds are filtered out.
                                         Subsequently, patches are assembled into 2 × 2 mosaic images. A penalty mechanism
                                   is applied to patches containing only objects from scale groups that have already reached
                                   the target count, reducing their selection priority during mosaic construction. This process
                                   is repeated until all scale groups reach the target object count, resulting in scale-balanced
                                   mosaic images and adjusted annotations for training.
